STHITHAPRAGYA A GAME OF CHANCE

STHITHAPRAGYA

स्थितप्रज्ञ one of steady mind

A shoot-the-balloon stall, the kind that stands at the edge of every carnival, promising a prize for a steady hand. The balloons carry words. Read across the wall, they say one sentence twice: I — am — not — happy — I — am — not — unhappy.

Before shooting, the player chooses a position. I am happy. I am not happy. I am unhappy. I am not unhappy. Whatever they already hold to be true about themselves that day. Then they take aim, trying to burst those words in a line, in order, so that the wall will say their sentence back to them.

They do not manage it. The barrel pulls, the wall is crowded, the balloons are not where the sentence needs them to be. A word goes that they wanted to keep. A word stays that they meant to lose. What is left standing reads as something they never claimed — and by the rules of the game, for that moment, it is their state.

The failure is the work.

We spend a good deal of life doing exactly this: gathering the evidence that corroborates the story we are already telling about ourselves, then trying to arrange the world into agreement. The arrangement does not hold. It was never ours to arrange. Things will not always go our way — and the day they don't, we are asked to call ourselves unhappy.

The Bhagavad Gita has a word for the one who is not thrown by this: sthitaprajña, of steady wisdom. Not the person who has won the swing between the pairs, but the one who has stopped being moved by it. Not numb. Not indifferent. Simply no longer at the mercy of which way the sentence falls.

The title is that person's answer. Not happy. Not unhappy. Two refusals, and the ease that lives between them.

At a carnival, in the middle of the noise, with a rifle in your hands and rubber bursting around you, this arrives as a joke first and a recognition later. Which is, more or less, how it arrives in a life.
